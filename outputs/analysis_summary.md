## 1. What was analysed

- **Observations:** 487,914 reported crashes
- **Date range:** 2021-01-01 to 2025-12-31
- **Years:** 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025 (complete calendar years only)
- **Injury crashes (>=1 person injured):** 195,275 (40.02% of reported crashes)
- **Fatal crashes (>=1 person killed):** 1,304 (2.67 per 1,000 reported crashes)
- **People injured:** 261,634
- **People killed:** 1,364

2026 is excluded: the published data end mid-2026, and a partial year would
bias any seasonal or annual comparison.

Reported crash volume falls steadily across the window (110,558 in 2021 to 85,546 in 2025, 23% lower), while the *share* of crashes involving an injury rises. Both trends are real in the
reported data, but neither can be read as a change in road risk without
exposure and reporting-practice information the dataset does not contain.

## 4. Data-quality finding: the `00:00` placeholder

`crash_time` is never null, but exactly-midnight is recorded 8,527 times (1.75% of all crashes) — roughly twice as often as any other single clock minute.

| Subset | n | Injury-crash rate | Fatal crashes | Fatal per 1,000 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| hour 0, all records | 22,133 | 38.46% | 70 | 3.16 |
| hour 0, exactly 00:00 only | 8,527 | 38.61% | 1 | 0.12 |
| hour 0, excluding 00:00 | 13,606 | 38.37% | 69 | 5.07 |
| all hours, all records | 487,914 | 40.02% | 1,304 | 2.67 |
| all hours, excluding 00:00 | 479,387 | 40.05% | 1,303 | 2.72 |

**This is the decisive evidence.** Records stamped exactly `00:00` contain 1 fatal crash in 8,527 records. Every neighbouring hour of the night runs 50-70 fatal crashes on a smaller base. A genuine hour of the night cannot
look like that, so `00:00` is behaving as a default value written when the true
time was not known.

**Decision: flag, do not drop.** Their injury rate (38.61%) is close to the rest of hour 0 (38.37%), so the injury analysis is barely affected, and dropping 1.75% of the data non-randomly would cost more than it buys.
The distortion is concentrated in one statistic — the hour-0 **fatality** rate, which
reads 3.16 per 1,000 with these records and 5.07 without them. **Figures showing fatality by hour exclude the flagged records and say so.**

## 5. Strongest descriptive patterns

### 5.1 Three different clocks — the central finding

| Question | Peak | Value | Trough | Value |
|---|---|---:|---|---:|
| When do crashes happen most? | **5 PM-6 PM** | 30,591 crashes | 3 AM-4 AM | 8,678 |
| When is a crash most likely to injure someone? | **9 PM-10 PM** | 45.3% | 2 AM-3 AM | 33.3% |
| When is a crash most likely to be fatal? | **3 AM-4 AM** | 7.14 per 1,000 | 4 PM-5 PM | 1.08 |

**The hypothesis in the project brief is supported, but not in the direction most
people expect.** Crash frequency and injury-crash rate do peak at different times
— frequency at 5 PM, injury share about 4 hours later at 9 PM. But the quiet overnight hours are **not** the times when a crash is most likely to
hurt someone. They are the times when it is *least* likely to:
33.3% at 2 AM-3 AM against 45.3% at 9 PM-10 PM.

The reversal appears when severity is measured by death instead of injury.
Overnight crashes are the **most** likely to be fatal: 7.14 per 1,000 at 3 AM-4 AM versus 1.08 at 4 PM-5 PM — a **6.6x** difference.

*Sample-size caution:* the fatal-crash comparison rests on 1,304 fatal crashes spread across 24 hours (62 in the peak hour, 33 in the trough). The 95% Wilson intervals for the peak (5.58-9.15) and trough (0.77-1.52) do not overlap, so the
contrast is real, but hour-to-hour wobble in the fatal series should not be
over-read. Grouped into time periods (section 5.3) it is much more stable.

### 5.7 Does the mix of road users explain the evening peak?

Partly. Holding aside every crash in which a pedestrian or cyclist was hurt, the
injury-crash rate among the remaining crashes still swings from 25.8% at 10 AM-11 AM to 34.8% at 9 PM-10 PM — a 9.1-point spread against 12.0 points for all crashes.

So roughly **24% of the hourly swing in the injury-crash rate
disappears** once pedestrian and cyclist casualties are set aside, and the rest
remains. The evening peak is therefore *both* a composition effect (more of the
evening's crashes hurt people outside vehicles) *and* a real shift within
vehicle-occupant crashes. Neither explanation alone is sufficient, and the
write-up should say so.

## 6. Hypothesis verdict

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Crash frequency and crash severity peak at different times | **Supported.** Frequency peaks 5 PM; injury share peaks 9 PM; fatality rate peaks 3 AM. |
| The quiet overnight hours are when crashes are most likely to injure | **Rejected.** They have the *lowest* injury-crash rate of the day. |
| 'Severity' is a single thing | **Rejected.** Injury-crash rate and fatality rate peak ~18 hours apart and rank the day almost oppositely. |
| Day of week is a major driver | **Not supported as a headline.** Real but small (2.9 points) next to time of day. |

## 7. Findings deliberately NOT emphasised

| Finding | Why it is held back |
|---|---|
| Borough differences | `borough` is 30% missing and almost certainly not missing at random. Reported as supporting context only, never as a ranking of boroughs. |
| Contributing factors | The most common recorded value is `Unspecified`. These are administrative classifications entered by a reporting officer, not established causes. No causal claim is made from them. |
| Vehicle types | Free-text-ish field with many near-duplicate spellings. Not cleaned to a standard taxonomy, so not used for any claim. |
| Hour-to-hour wobble in the fatal series | Individual hours hold only 29-76 fatal crashes. Only the broad overnight-vs-afternoon contrast is claimed, not specific hour rankings. |
| Year-over-year decline in crashes | Real in the reported data, but reporting practice and traffic volumes both changed across 2021-2025. Shown for context; not interpreted as a safety improvement. |
| Any per-trip risk comparison between road users | Impossible without exposure data. See section 8. |

## 8. Limitations

1. **Reported crashes are not all crashes.** These are NYPD-reported collisions. New York requires a report for injury, death, or property damage above a threshold, so minor collisions are under-represented — and probably unevenly across time of day. If a fender-bender at 3 AM with no one around is less likely to be reported than the same crash at 3 PM, the overnight denominator shrinks, which would push the overnight injury *rate* up. The observed overnight injury rate is the lowest of the day **despite** that pressure, which makes the finding more robust, not less.
2. **No exposure denominator.** The dataset counts crashes, not trips, vehicle-miles, or people walking and cycling. Every rate here is conditional on a crash having been reported. Nothing supports a statement like 'driving at 2 AM is more dangerous' or 'cyclists are more likely to be hurt'.
3. **Recorded contributing factor is not established cause.** See section 7.
4. **Missing data are not random.** Borough and coordinates are missing together on ~30% and ~8% of records respectively; highway crashes disproportionately lack a borough.
5. **Timestamp quality.** See section 4.
6. **Road-user flags are circular by construction.** Every `pedestrian_injury_crash` is an `injury_crash`, because the flag is built from pedestrian casualty counts. Injury rates must never be compared *between* road-user flags. Shares of all reported crashes are the safe comparison, and are what is used.
